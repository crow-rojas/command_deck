1. Learn by Watching.
One of the best ways to learn cooking is to observe and imitate. If
you want to be better at cooking (and anything, in general), watch
people do it. This can be as simple as putting on a cooking tv show
while you eat, watching YouTube videos of chefs explaining their
processes, etc. No matter your level of competence, you will grow your
skillset much more quickly if you are watching and learning from
people who've made it.
2. Get the Right Tools.
Most of the recipes in this book require very basic equipment.
Here are some examples:
12-inch non-stick pan
A sharp chef's knife
A small paring knife
Cutting board

Blender
Microwave
Crock Pot or slow cooker
- Measuring cups or bowls
You don't need a 24-piece kitchen set with 9 different sizes of pans.
You need a couple good ones at most. Focus on the basics.

3. Stop Measuring. Start Eyeballing.
I barely measure anything I cook. And it's not because I have some 6th
sense for measurements. I've just found that the low-measurement
strategy creates the best cooking experience possible.
Measuring out everything takes work and is exhausting. It creates
extra dishes and can be the reason why a recipe takes twice as long to
make. Longer term, it prevents you from developing your own personal
sense of volume, which is key to unlocking your creativity in the
kitchen.
Don't get me wrong - I do measure certain things. Baking for instance,
is a lot more about precise chemistry, so l'll measure out the flour.
Sauces l'll be a little more tidy on as well since the proportions are
pretty crucial to the final product. I always make sure that core
ingredients are measured more or less to the amount I need.
But with most recipes, you'll find that there's a lot of latitude in the
measurements. Seasonings, for instance, are almost never worth
grabbing measuring spoons for. You shouldn't be pouring out a
teaspoon of salt and pepper for your chicken seasoning - a few twists
of your pepper grinder should be enough. And if it isn't, you can always
add more.

Cooking gets frustrating when you obsess over every fine grained
detail. Don't! I certainly recommend measuring most things at the start
to give yourself a feel, but then consciously practice eyeballing
amounts to see where that gets you. Measuring everything is like
training wheels on a bicycle: your goal is to reach a point where you no
longer need them.
Look for opportunities to eyeball ingredients to your personal taste,
such as spinach, shredded cheese, and garlic. Trial and error is one of
the best teachers you have!
Once you're at a point where you can eyeball 70% of the ingredient
measurements, you can make food FAST. You can also customize it to
your liking, and eventually, make new recipes of your own. My Shredded
Jalapeño Ranch recipe originally called for one jalapeño, but one day I
threw in two to see the difference. Then three. Three was the sweet
spot - and now that recipe is locked.
4. Season and Taste as You Go.
You get to decide what your food tastes like. To get familiar with
flavors, practice seasoning with a little before adding more. Salt &
pepper are found in TONS of recipes - they are an excellent foundation
on which to add other flavors.

One of the things that distinguishes cooking from baking is that
baking is a lot more about precise chemistry. Measurements are really
important - a bit too much flour can ruin the whole recipe. Cooking has
a lot more flexibility! You can add or adjust seasonings as you go to
create the flavor you like most. Don't hesitate to improvise and make
food taste the way you want it to.
For instance, with my Rosemary Garlic Sirloin Roast, I'lllet it cook
through the crock pot and then take a fork to taste one of the
potatoes and carrots. Based on that, I'll add some more pepper, or salt,
etc. I won't stop until I love the bite I just took - then it's serving time!
This also reduces the pressure of cooking. If you're making food for
other people, it's natural to have some nerves about screwing up and
serving mediocre food. Seasoning and tasting as you go is the antidote.
Don't serve the food until it personally satisfies you.
5. Make Your Leftovers Taste Like Day One.
The secret to great meal prep is knowing how to reheat your foop
properly. That's why I've included a simple reheating guide for every
recipe that needs it. My preferred tools are the microwave (to conserve
moisture) and air fryer (to re-crisp).
If you don't have an air fryer, don't worry - your oven will work great.
Just add 25°F (15°C) to the temperature and about 20% to the time.
For example, if an instruction calls for 10 minutes at 375°F (190°C) in
the air fryer, you'll set your oven to 40O°F (205°C) and start checking
your food around the 12-minute mark. It's that easy.

6. Focus on Calories, Protein, Price, and Prep Time.
You'll notice that this cookbook lists some important stats at the top
of each recipe. There are lots of elements to a recipe I've chosen not to
include, such as Vitamin A and zinc. There's a reason for this.
Why Calories?
Net calories determine changes in your weight. If you want to gain
weight, you need to eat more calories than you take in, aka a caloric
surplus. If you want to lose weight, you need to burn off more calories
than you take in, aka a caloric deficit. Including calories in each recipe
gives you insight into how your meals effect your weight.
Why Protein?
Proteins are one of three macronutrients, the other two being
carbohydrates and fats. Proteins are essential for muscle growth - no
matter how hard you train, if you're not eating adequate protein, you
won't build muscle. Many people are using this cookbook to get in
shape, transform their physiques, and become stronger. We have to
measure and track protein to get there.
Why Price?
Saving money is a crucial component of home cooking. The
calculations used here are rough and vary dramatically depending on
where you live, where you shop for groceries, and how much you buy in
bulk. Regardless, even a general estimate of price per serving is useful
because it helps you keep your costs in check.

Why Prep Time?
Your time is valuable. Including prep time means you can know
roughly how long it will take to make each meal because some take
more time than others. Smoothies are always the fastest - go there for
true 5-minute meals. Most recipes in here can be made in under 30
minutes, and that includes cooking time (such as the 15 minutes it
takes for chicken to air fry). To maximize your time, use cooking or
baking time to clean up your station. There's nothing better than
enjoying hot food in a clean kitchen.
Why Carbs and Fats?
All macronutrients are important, but not all of them need close
attention. The numbers are provided for people eating specialized diets
that track these macros more specifically.
There are people who will track every atom of their food and swear on
their mother's life that knowing it is essential for their physique. It's
just not true. Of course there are always exceptions in niche scenarios,
but for the average person, knowing your calories and protein will be
enough to get in shape, lose weight, and achieve your goals. That's
what we're here to do.
Don't neglect getting in adequate carbs and fats - and generally, l'd
recommend avoiding diets that push extreme restrictions ("avoid all
carbs! avoid all fats!"). You can get your micronutrients in by eating
small number of vegetables or by taking a greens powder or other
vitamins. It's not that hard to do.

A NOTE ON
MEASUREMENTS
If a recipe says "4x" next to an ingredient, that means you'll use 4 of
that item.
Throughout this book, you'll see ingredients listed in two systems:
Left side: Metric (grams)
Right side: US Customary (cups, tablespoons, etc)
If you grew up in the U.S. like me, you probably default to cups and
spoons. It's familiar, and it does the job. That said, it can't compete
with the precision and simplicity of the metric system.
Here's why: cups and spoons measure volume, while grams measure
weight. Volume can shift depending on how you scoop or pack an
ingredient, but weight never lies. If you care about hitting your calories
and protein accurately, grams are the way to go.
All you need to go metric is a cheap digital food scale. To use it, just
put your mixing bowl on the scale and hit TARE. This resets the weight
to zero so the scale ignores the weight of the empty bowl. Then add
your ingredients directly into the bowl - no guesswork, no extra
measuring cups to wash, and perfectly accurate results every time.
